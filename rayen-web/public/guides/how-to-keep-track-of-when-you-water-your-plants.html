How to keep track of when you water your plants

By Francisco Arrieta · 13 November 2026 · 4 min read

Most of us do not forget to water our plants. We forget when we last did. Did the fern get a drink on Sunday, or was that the monstera? A small record answers the question, and over a few weeks it teaches you more about your plants than any care label.

What is worth writing down

  • The plant and the date you watered it. That is the whole record. Everything else is optional.
  • How dry the soil was, if you want one more thing. A word is enough: dry, damp, soaked.
  • Anything unusual: a drooping leaf, a new pot, a move to a brighter window.

Resist recording amounts at first. “Watered” is enough for most houseplants, and a detailed log you stop keeping is worth less than a simple one you keep.

Four ways to keep the record

On paper

A notebook page, or a printed chart taped inside a cupboard door, with plant names down the side and dates across the top. It costs nothing, needs no battery and works in any house. The catch is that it only helps if it is where you are when you water, and that tends to be wherever you left it. Paper suits a handful of plants in one room.

Your phone’s calendar or reminders

A repeating event for each plant (“water the fern, every 7 days”) will nudge you at the right time. The weakness is that the schedule is a guess. When you water a day late, or skip because the soil is still damp, the event does not know, so it nags you on the wrong days and after a few weeks you stop listening. One event per plant also gets noisy quickly.

A spreadsheet or a notes app

A row per plant with a column for the last watered date gives you something you can sort and glance at. It is flexible and free. The cost is effort: you have to open it, find the row and type a date every time, and that friction is why most of these lists die in the third week.

A plant app

An app can keep the dates, sort by which plant is thirstiest and sometimes nudge you. Apps differ in ways that matter, so check before you commit:

  • Does it need an account?
  • Where does your data live, and can you export it so it is not lost with the phone?
  • Does it work without a signal?
  • Is the price a one-off, a subscription or advertising?

The date matters more than the schedule

A care label that says “water every week” is an average of many homes and no one’s. What a plant needs depends on its light, its pot, the season and the room. The one thing you can know for certain is when you last watered it.

Write that down and the schedule can be corrected by what actually happens. If a plant is always thirsty after five days, set it to five. If the soil is still wet on day seven, push the next watering back. Plants generally drink less in winter, when the light is lower and growth slows, so a rhythm that worked in summer often needs stretching. A record lets you see that pattern happen instead of leaving you to remember it.

Check the soil as well

A record tells you when you last watered. It cannot see the soil. Push a finger two or three centimetres (about an inch) into the pot: if it is still damp, wait; if it is dry, water. Treat the schedule as the question “is it time to check?” and the soil as the answer.

A routine that sticks

Pick one day a week. Walk the room, check each plant’s soil, water the ones that are dry and note the date for those. It takes five minutes. Doing it on a set day means you are never relying on memory for the checking, only for the recording, and the recording is the easy part.

Where Rayen fits

Rayen is a small web app I made for exactly this. You add your plants and how often each one likes water. The deck puts the plant that needs you most at the front, and you turn the card over and press Water to write the date down. It has no account and nothing to install, and your garden stays on your own device.

It does not send reminders. The deck is the reminder: you open it and the plant that is next is in front. If you want notifications, a calendar will do that better, and the two work well together.
